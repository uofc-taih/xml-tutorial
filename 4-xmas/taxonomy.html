23  Taxonomy

23.1 What are agents?

Generally, agents refer to any entity capable of perceiving its environment and taking action (Cheng et al. 2024). Namely, large language model (LLM) based agents leverage the natural language processing capabilities of LLMs (complex reasoning, text summarization, machine translation) (Qin et al. 2026) , while attempting to address their fundamental limitations: (Plaat et al. 2025; Liu and Shu 2025)

  1. Hallucination The tendency of LLMs to produce plausible yet factually unsupported content, deriving from data misinformation, pre-training biases, and over-confidence, among others. (Huang et al. 2025)

  2. Training data LLM intelligence is limited by the data available during training, which causes struggles in factual accuracy and failure to adapt to new situations.

  3. Reasoning Although LLMs continue to impress in natural language processing, model reasoning remains a well-reported challenge as LLMs are designed to predict the most probable next words, rather than making logically sound claims.

  4. Prompt Engineering LLMs are sensitive to minor details in prompts, making prompt improvement a tedious, manual task. (Plaat et al. 2025)

Consequently, the architecture of LLM-based agents adds four fundamental modules absent from LLMs: (Wang et al. 2023)

Profile: The identity, role, tendencies, and constraints that define an agent and lead to better, grounded decisions.

Memory: The persistent system by which agents retain, refine, and recall information while accumulating knowledge.

Planning: The module that decomposes complex, high-level plans into sub-tasks for a simplified sequence of actions.

Action: The tools that enable an agent to translate internal decisions into external executions.

Some authors argue for a fifth module evolution, describing how agents progressively enhance their intelligence through self-reflection. (Li et al. 2024)

Analogously, agents can be understood as being proactive whereas LLMs are reactive. Rather, LLMs predict and agents reason, act and interact. (Plaat et al. 2025)

By adopting a proactive stance, LLM-based agents address the constraints of models by integrating a model within an architectural loop with distinct modules that handle it’s limitations. (Liu and Shu 2025; Plaat et al. 2025)

Implementations of agent-based systems have rapidly evolved into a variety of industries, (arguably) justifying it’s position as the current paradigm in machine learning research. Such areas include healthcare (medical diagnosis (J. Chen et al. 2025), cardiology (Zhou et al. 2024), therapeutic counseling (Steenstra and Bickmore 2025)), research (survey generation (Liang et al. 2025), scientific discovery (Gottweis and Natarajan 2025)), and software engineering (coding reasoning (H.-Y. Chen et al. 2025), code generation (Endla et al. 2025)). The term agent has even become a buzzword within the tech industry, often being misused by marketing teams to excite unbeknownst customers, reflecting the prominence of single-agent systems. (Cheng et al. 2024; Ferrag et al. 2026)

But as the scopes and complexities of single-agent implementations grow, the more difficult is it for an agent to navigate more dynamic, complex scenarios. An underlying assumption within an abundance of single-agent implementations is static, closed environments, which, of course, is a reductive representation of nuanced, real-world scenarios. (Qi et al. 2026; Pan and Wu 2025; Feng et al. 2026)

23.1.1 What are multi-agent systems?

Multi-agent systems (MAS) are systems that enclose multiple autonomous LLM-based agents to enable more complex reasoning, problem-solving, and simulation. Leveraging collective judgment is a natural step in the evolution of agentic AI, addressing the simplistic assumed environments of single-agent systems. As singular agents reach limits in complexity, they may relapse to the initial pitfalls of LLMs (hallucination, limits of reasoning). Containing multiple agents in a cohesive framework allows for dynamic collaboration, flexibility, and emergent collective intelligence. (Guo et al. 2024; Pan and Wu 2025) As Aristotle argued, “a feast to which many contribute is better than a dinner provided out of a single purse”. (Aristotle 1885)

Unlike the evolution of reactive LLMs to proactive LLM-based agents, the progression of single-agent systems to multi-agent systems is more so a re-contextualization than an evolution. Multi-agent systems typically avoid heavily altering the architecture of single-agents, rather enabling it’s modules (profiling, memory, planning, action) to adequately handle a multi-agent context through inter-agent means. (Guo et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024)

Notably, agents within MAS are more specialized; agent profiles can, for example, contain human personas (Park et al. 2023) or possess knowledge limited to specific disciplines (Tang et al. 2024) to simulate real-world contexts or solve complex tasks. Similarly, planning modules and action modules are equipped with unique mechanisms that address multi-agent complexity, namely, task decomposition and collaborative feedback loops for planning (Li et al. 2025), and environmental interaction for action. Memory modules extend further: hybrid memory provides agents with shared memory, capturing emergent collective intelligence. (Wu and Shu 2026) More exhaustive, MAS architecture surveys elaborate on the methodologies used to handle MAS complexities in distributed knowledge, coordination, communication, among others. (Guo et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024)

Beyond refactoring single-agent architectures, orchestration represents the novel mechanisms by which agents are integrated within an MAS, re-contextualizing agents to adequately perform under more complex and nuanced scenarios. (Adimulam et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026)

Orchestration can be understood as the structured coordination and collaboration of enclosed agents. (Adimulam et al. 2026) Orchestration is defined by three key dimensions, organizational structure, communication mechanism, and collaboration pattern, through which various MAS can address unique use cases. (Adimulam et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026)

Organizational Structure: Defined as the interaction topology of the MAS, which outlines how authority is divided and which agents interact directly . Among others, some structures are:

Hierarchical: A pyramidal chain, akin to a traditional top-down business model. Higher tier agents interact with agents immediately below them, who subsequently continue the chain of command.

Decentralized: Agents communicate with other agents directly, removing any need for a central coordinator. Uniquely, every agent is able to coordinate with any other agent.

Flat Collaborative: Authority is distributed among all agents, but any agent can only communicate with those near it.

Communication Mechanism: Procedures and protocols used to transmit and synchronize information, including intent, observations, and tasks. Some forms include:

Direct Channels: Communication by which information is explicitly relayed towards another agent, or a group of agents.

Indirect Channels: Receivers and senders are not explicitly determined during communication, rather, communication involves modifying a shared state.

Collaboration Pattern: The methodologies by which agents sequence tasks, distribute responsibilities, and provide feedback. An MAS collaboration pattern defines how each agent works together, utilizing it’s structure and communication methods. The key dimensions are the following.

Collaborative intent: The relationship between the agents’ objectives, defining whether agents pursue a shared positive-sum objective, or mutually competing on a micro-level for a macro-level, aligned goal.

Collaborative workflows: The process through which information and tasks are advanced among agents, ranging from feed-forward pipelines, or parallel, aggregation.

Collaborative protocols: The standards that define interactions for communication. (Zhou et al. 2026)

Orchestration has been proven to be a necessary aspect for scalable MAS, preventing drawbacks such as conflicting goals, circular reasoning, or inconsistent memories which emerge from evolving past single-agent systems. Combined with re-contextualized agent architectures, orchestration helps MAS tackle real-world, complex tasks. (Dextra Labs 2026)

23.1.2 Why is explainability in multi-agent systems necessary?

The autonomy and powerful reasoning of agentic systems can easily be seen as a century-defining technology, grasping at the possibilities of superhuman cognitive ability. However, the excitement behind agentic AI, including the growing academic research and commercialization, may be propagating its inherent opacity, the fatal flaw of agentic systems. In the general scope of LLMs, the rapidly growing gap between the ability to interpret models and the widespread usage of these models is called the explainability crisis. (Decision Physics 2026)

The difficulty of interpreting LLMs (as discussed in previous chapters) can be attributed to its opaque systems. As a result, agentic systems that inherit LLMs as fundamental mechanisms would inherit a potentially unintelligible model. Multi-agent systems amplifies this effect by introducing new dimensions to agentic systems, complicating explainability moreover. The most notable reasons are:

  1. Distributed information obscures attribution: Information parsed and refined between multiple agents in systematic procedures distorts the interpretability of intentions, decisions, and behaviours by introducing layers of abstraction.

  2. Cascading errors and behaviours among agents: The degradation of communication introduces errors and unwanted behaviours that cascades into system-level effects, making failure attribution difficult. (Tanveer 2026)

  1. The orchestration layer introduces complexity: Orchestration, beyond adding structural integrity and coordination, also adds a layer of complexity that disrupts causal attribution. (Adimulam et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026)

Although the explainability crisis may be generally applicable to the broader scope of black-box models, multi-agent systems experience this effect amplified. Concerns raise as MAS emerge as viable options to SAS, as to address more specialized challenges.

Notably, Anthropic utilizes MAS in the Claude Research feature, arguing for it’s effectiveness in open-ended research questions. (Hadfield et al. 2025) The idea that the gradual commercialization of MAS necessitates explainability is greater than the field of ML; after all, any technology implemented without systemic understanding seems to be unethical.
